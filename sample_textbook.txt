Chapter 1: Introduction to Machine Learning

Machine learning is a branch of artificial intelligence that allows computer
systems to improve their performance on a task by learning from data, rather
than being explicitly programmed with fixed rules. Instead of a human writing
every decision rule by hand, the system is shown many examples and learns a
pattern from them on its own.

There are three broad categories of machine learning. In supervised learning,
the model is trained on labeled data, meaning every example comes with the
correct answer attached, and the model learns to map inputs to outputs. In
unsupervised learning, the data has no labels, and the model instead looks
for structure or grouping in the data on its own, such as clustering similar
customers together. In reinforcement learning, an agent learns by interacting
with an environment, receiving rewards or penalties for its actions, and
gradually improving its strategy over time.

Chapter 2: The Bias-Variance Tradeoff

A central challenge in machine learning is finding a model that generalizes
well to new, unseen data rather than just memorizing the training data. Bias
refers to error introduced by overly simplistic assumptions in the model,
which can cause it to miss important patterns, a problem known as underfitting.
Variance refers to error introduced by a model that is too sensitive to small
fluctuations in the training data, which can cause it to fit noise instead of
the true underlying pattern, a problem known as overfitting.

The goal of model selection is to find a sweet spot between bias and variance.
A model with high bias and low variance is simple and stable but inaccurate.
A model with low bias and high variance is flexible and accurate on training
data but unreliable on new data. Techniques such as cross-validation, and
regularization methods like L1 and L2 penalties, help strike this balance.

Chapter 3: Neural Networks and Deep Learning

An artificial neural network is loosely inspired by the structure of the
human brain. It consists of layers of small computational units called
neurons, connected to one another by weighted connections. Data enters
through an input layer, passes through one or more hidden layers where it is
transformed, and produces a result at the output layer. This process of
passing data forward through the network is called forward propagation.

During training, the network compares its prediction to the correct answer
using a loss function, which measures how wrong the prediction was. It then
uses an algorithm called backpropagation, combined with gradient descent, to
adjust the weights of every connection slightly in the direction that reduces
the error. Repeating this process over many examples and many passes, called
epochs, gradually improves the network's accuracy.

Convolutional neural networks, or CNNs, are a specialized type of neural
network designed for grid-like data such as images. They use filters that
slide across the input to detect local patterns like edges and textures,
making them far more efficient than a standard fully-connected network for
visual tasks. Recurrent neural networks, or RNNs, are instead designed for
sequential data such as text or time series, since they maintain a form of
memory of previous inputs as they process a sequence step by step.

Chapter 1: Introduction to Computer Networks

A computer network is a collection of interconnected devices that can
exchange data with one another. Networks are typically classified by their
geographic scope. A Local Area Network, or LAN, connects devices within a
single building or campus, such as computers in a college lab. A Wide Area
Network, or WAN, spans a much larger geographic area, connecting multiple
LANs across cities or countries, with the internet being the largest
example of a WAN.

Data travels across a network in small units called packets. Rather than
sending an entire file as one continuous stream, the sending device breaks
it into packets, each carrying a small piece of the data along with header
information such as the source address, destination address, and a
sequence number so the packets can be reassembled correctly at the other
end, even if they arrive out of order.

Chapter 2: The OSI and TCP/IP Models

The OSI model describes networking as seven layers, each responsible for a
specific job: physical, data link, network, transport, session,
presentation, and application. Data passed down from the application layer
gets wrapped with additional header information at each layer below it, a
process called encapsulation, and the reverse process, decapsulation,
happens at the receiving end as the data moves back up through the layers.

In practice, most real networks use the simpler four-layer TCP/IP model:
network access, internet, transport, and application. The Internet
Protocol, or IP, operates at the internet layer and is responsible for
addressing and routing packets across networks. The Transmission Control
Protocol, or TCP, operates at the transport layer and guarantees reliable,
ordered delivery by requiring the receiver to acknowledge each packet, and
retransmitting any packet that gets lost. The User Datagram Protocol, or
UDP, is a faster but unreliable alternative at the same layer, used when
speed matters more than guaranteed delivery, such as in live video calls.

Chapter 3: Routing and Switching

A switch operates within a single network and forwards data based on
physical hardware addresses called MAC addresses, learning which device is
connected to which port by observing traffic. A router, by contrast,
connects multiple different networks together and forwards data based on
IP addresses, using a routing table to decide the best path for a packet
to reach its destination network.

Routing protocols determine how routers build and update these tables.
Distance-vector protocols, such as RIP, have each router share its own
routing table with its immediate neighbors, gradually building a picture
of the wider network. Link-state protocols, such as OSPF, instead have
every router build a complete map of the network's topology by flooding
information about its direct connections to all other routers, then
independently calculating the shortest path to every destination.
